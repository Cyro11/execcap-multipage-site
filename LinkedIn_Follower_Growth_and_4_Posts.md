# ExecCap Advisors — LinkedIn Follower Growth Direction & Four Post Concepts

## Objective
Increase follows on the ExecCap Advisors Company Page by making a defined audience—experienced executives considering ownership—want to see the next point of view. The operating test is not raw impressions alone: it is follows, qualified comments, profile visits, and conversations from executives, owners, and capital partners.

## What to take from the Maxwell Salazar direction
The useful adaptation is **not** generic meme posting. It is a repeatable combination of:

1. a familiar business visual or cultural tension that stops the scroll;
2. one sharp, defensible operator point of view;
3. a prompt that makes the right people choose a side or share experience; and
4. enough specificity that readers know why to follow the page.

This should be adapted to ExecCap’s brand book: authoritative, strategic, established, disciplined, and trustworthy. Humor should point at acquisition myths, bad process, or the distance between spreadsheet logic and operating reality—never at business owners, job loss, or deal counterparties.

## Recommended content system: “The Ownership Reality Check”
- **Cadence:** 1 high-conviction discussion post weekly + 1 evidence-led educational post weekly.
- **Visual pattern:** restrained navy/white meme tile or simple two-panel editorial chart; one direct headline; no stock “corporate handshake” image.
- **Caption pattern:** pointed first line → 2–4 lines of practical explanation → one precise question → “Follow ExecCap Advisors for operator-first ETA perspectives.”
- **Comment handling:** reply to every substantive comment within one business day with a real point of view—not a generic thank-you. Tag no one unless their contribution genuinely expands the discussion.
- **Follower conversion:** use the Company Page name in every post’s final line, use a recurring series name, and make the post useful enough that the next installment feels worth following.
- **Guardrails:** no financial promises, valuations, unnamed deal anecdotes presented as fact, or invented transaction data. Do not use engagement bait such as “comment YES.”

## Four potential posts

### 1) The “Great Deal / Terrible Job” meme
**Primary audience:** experienced executives considering ETA

**Visual concept:** two-panel hard-edge graphic.
- Left: `THE SPREADSHEET: 5.0x EBITDA. “Great deal.”`
- Right: `MONDAY MORNING: 63 employees, 2 key customers, no operating leader. “Now what?”`
- Footer: `A deal is not an acquisition thesis until you can lead it.`

**Post copy:**
> A business can be attractively priced and still be a terrible business for *you* to buy.
>
> The spreadsheet may show a clean multiple. But the first Monday after close asks different questions:
> - Can you lead this team?
> - Can you earn the trust of its customers?
> - Can you operate through the issues that do not fit in the model?
>
> In ETA, the buyer is part of the underwriting.
>
> What is one “great deal” characteristic that becomes a red flag when you picture yourself running the business?
>
> Follow ExecCap Advisors for operator-first perspectives on ownership through acquisition.

**Why it can travel:** recognizably true tension, easy-to-share visual, and a question that rewards experienced comments.

---

### 2) The pointed view: “No one buys a spreadsheet”
**Primary audience:** acquisition entrepreneurs and searchers

**Visual concept:** navy title slide with a simple three-line contrast.
- `You can diligence EBITDA.`
- `You can reference-check a customer.`
- `You cannot outsource the first 100 days.`

**Post copy:**
> Unpopular opinion: the most important question in a small-business acquisition is not “What multiple did you pay?”
>
> It is: **why are you the person who should own and lead this particular business?**
>
> Capital can help close a transaction. Advisors can help pressure-test it. Neither replaces operating credibility after the announcement.
>
> The strongest ownership theses connect three things:
> 1. a business with durable reasons to exist;
> 2. a transition the team and customers can believe in; and
> 3. an operator with a real reason to be there.
>
> Which is harder to underwrite: the company or the future owner?
>
> Follow ExecCap Advisors for the ownership questions that come before the term sheet.

**Why it can travel:** a contrarian but professional opening sets up a binary discussion without reducing the subject to a hot take.

---

### 3) The “deal process vs. actual life” meme
**Primary audience:** senior executives evaluating a career transition

**Visual concept:** two-column calendar comparison.
- Left: `DEAL TIMELINE: LOI → diligence → close`
- Right: `OWNER TIMELINE: first all-hands → first customer issue → first payroll → first hard decision`
- Headline: `Close is a date. Ownership is a job.`

**Post copy:**
> The deal process trains people to focus on closing.
>
> Ownership rewards people who prepare for what comes after it.
>
> A transition plan should not begin after the wire moves. Before close, a prospective owner should be thinking about:
> - the first all-hands meeting;
> - which customer relationships need personal attention;
> - the decisions that cannot wait for a 100-day plan; and
> - where the predecessor’s knowledge is actually concentrated.
>
> Close is a date. Ownership is a job.
>
> What would you want to understand before Day One that a standard diligence checklist might miss?
>
> Follow ExecCap Advisors for practical ETA and ownership-transition thinking.

**Why it can travel:** it replaces generic “post-close value creation” language with a familiar operating reality that prompts leaders to add practical examples.

---

### 4) The seller-transition view: “If it only works with a perfect handoff, it may not work”
**Primary audience:** business owners and acquisition entrepreneurs

**Visual concept:** simple risk meter with five inputs: customer relationships, employee trust, seller knowledge, systems, decision rights. Headline: `A transition plan is part of the deal—not a closing checklist.`

**Post copy:**
> A seller transition is often described in one sentence: “The owner will stay for 90 days.”
>
> That is not a transition plan.
>
> The hard questions are more specific:
> - Which relationships live with the owner?
> - What knowledge has never been documented?
> - Who does the team really go to when something breaks?
> - What does a credible successor need to earn before taking the wheel?
>
> If the acquisition only works with a perfect handoff, the handoff itself deserves more underwriting.
>
> Owners and operators: what is the most underestimated part of a leadership transition?
>
> Follow ExecCap Advisors for a disciplined view of operator-led succession and ETA.

**Why it can travel:** it invites both buyer and seller perspectives, giving the page access to two adjacent audiences without becoming vague.

## Sequencing recommendation
1. **Post 1 first** — broadest hook and clearest executive relevance.
2. **Post 3 next** — reinforces the operator-first positioning.
3. **Post 2** — establishes a higher-conviction brand voice after the page has earned context.
4. **Post 4** — widens the conversation to owners and makes the page more relevant to succession.

## Measurement cadence
For each post, record 7-day impressions, follows, profile visits, meaningful comments, shares, and inbound conversations. Use follows-per-1,000 impressions and meaningful-comments-per-1,000 impressions as directional diagnostics—not a single viral-post verdict. Keep the recurring topic/visual device only if it earns both reach and the right audience response.
