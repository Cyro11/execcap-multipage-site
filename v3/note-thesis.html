Search strategy

A useful thesis makes the search smaller.

Turn a broad interest into practical criteria for the business you want to own.

ExecCap Advisors · Field note

In this note

Operator fitBusiness criteriaPreferences & requirementsA working document

A search thesis is useful when it helps you make a decision. It should tell you where to look, what to prioritize, and which opportunities to decline. A long list of attractive industries rarely does that on its own.

Start with the operator.

Write down the environments you understand well: the customers you have served, the teams you have led, and the operating problems you can recognize. Consider where that experience would help you assess a business and where you would need support.

Translate experience into criteria.

Describe the characteristics of a business that would fit. These might include its customers, revenue model, geography, operating complexity, and dependence on the current owner. Explain why each criterion matters to your ability to lead the company.

Separate preferences from requirements.

A requirement should have a reason. A preference can change as you learn. Keeping them separate makes it easier to compare businesses without rewriting the thesis to fit every opportunity.

A practical starting exercise

Write one page with four parts: the business you seek, the reason you can lead it, the evidence you need, and the conditions that would make you walk away.

Make it a working document.

Test the thesis against actual opportunities. Record what you learn and revise the criteria deliberately. The aim is a focused search that benefits from experience without being redirected by every appealing listing.
