Opportunity evaluation

The first screen should surface the hard questions.

Look beyond the headline to the evidence that could change the acquisition decision.

ExecCap Advisors · Field note

In this note

Revenue qualityThe owner’s roleEarnings & cashDecision-changing evidence

An initial screen should help you decide what deserves further investigation. It should also identify the assumptions that make the opportunity attractive. If those assumptions cannot be tested, the screening process has left its most useful work unfinished.

Understand how revenue is earned.

Ask who buys, why they return, and what could make them leave. Look for the distinction between a recurring relationship and a revenue figure that only appears predictable in a summary.

Look at the owner’s role.

Identify the relationships and tasks that depend on the seller. Consider what would need to transfer and who could take responsibility. A strong business may still require a demanding transition.

Put cash alongside reported earnings.

Review working capital, capital expenditure, and the reporting information available. A headline earnings number does not explain all the demands on cash. Detailed conclusions require appropriate financial diligence.

Write down what could change your mind.

For each important claim, identify the information that would support or challenge it. That keeps the next conversation focused and makes it easier to distinguish a promising opportunity from an appealing narrative.

Before moving forward

Summarize the attraction, the three questions most likely to change the decision, and the evidence required to answer them. Assign a next step to each question.

A screen is a starting point. It helps you organize further work and decide whether the business merits the time and resources needed for diligence.
